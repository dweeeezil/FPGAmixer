FPGAmixer for sound designers

What is inside a mixer built on an FPGA, and why that matters for audio. No engineering background needed: every idea is explained with things you already know from sound work.

The one-minute version

FPGAmixer is a 20-in, 20-out digital mixer on a single chip. Audio arrives from a computer, from network audio or from a USB interface, gets mixed, and leaves the same ways. You control it from an app.

The mixing is not done by software. It is done by a circuit built inside an FPGA, so every sample follows exactly the same path in exactly the same time, and the chip keeps its own sample clock.

1What it does

It is a console's routing and faders, without the console.

Think of the two matrices as send grids. The first decides how much of each input goes to each mix (like aux and group sends). The second decides how much of each mix reaches each output. Settings are saved on the board and come back after a power cut.

2Two kinds of brain in one chip

The chip holds a small computer and a blank sheet of electronics, side by side.

The computer half

A small Linux computer (four ARM cores). It handles conversation: USB, network audio, the app, saving settings.

It follows instructions one after another, and it can be interrupted.

Like the computer running your DAW.

The FPGA half

A grid of tiny electronic building blocks and wires that you connect into the exact circuit you want. Nothing "runs". The design is the circuit.

Everything in it works at the same instant, on a precise schedule.

Like the circuitry in an analog console, but digital and rewirable.

Why this helps. When a DAW crackles at a very small buffer, the computer was late: some other task got in the way. A circuit has no other tasks. Each part does one job, every sample, forever. That is the main reason to build audio hardware this way.

Rule of thumb in this project: anything that must happen on every sample is in the FPGA. Anything that is conversation or bookkeeping is in the computer.

3Everything keeps time to one clock

You know word clock. Here the chip makes its own and counts it.

Digital audio only works if every part agrees when a sample happens. The chip's clock ticks 12,288,000 times a second. At 48 kHz that is exactly 256 ticks per sample. The designers count in ticks, so timing is arithmetic, not guesswork.

The chip can follow the network's clock

Network audio shares one clock across all devices (it is called gPTP). The board steers its own sample clock to match, in tiny steps. Two devices agree to within 3 to 4 billionths of a second, thousands of times finer than a single sample.

Where the delay really comes from

The mixing circuit itself adds about 20 millionths of a second. The delay you would actually hear today (about 61 ms for a round trip through USB) comes from buffers on the computer side, not from the FPGA.

4From the computer half to the FPGA half

Audio crosses over in small batches, on the FPGA's schedule.

This is the part that will feel most familiar, because it works like the I/O buffer in your audio settings.

The FPGA is in charge of timing

The courier fetches a sample when the chip's clock says one is due. The computer only has to keep the buffer topped up. If it ever runs late, the mixer outputs silence for that sample and counts it, rather than playing a wrong sample.

Why not feed the FPGA directly?

Because the two halves are separate clocks. A buffer between two clocks is the standard cure, the same reason a sample-rate mismatch needs a buffer or a converter. Here the queue handles it in hardware.

5Few workers, many jobs

The chip reuses a handful of calculators very fast, instead of building hundreds.

Mixing is multiplication: each sample times a gain. A 20-by-20 routing grid has 400 gains, so 400 multiplications per sample. Doing all 400 at once would need 400 calculators ("DSP slices"), but the chip only has 360, and each would sit idle almost all the time.

6Why use an FPGA for audio?

Timing you can count

The mix takes exactly 251 ticks, every sample, for the life of the product. There is no scheduler to be late.

Everything at once

All 20 channels, all routings and all levels are processed together, like channels on an analog console, but with digital precision.

It owns the clock

The sample clock is made inside the chip and can be steered to match the network, so no sample-rate conversion is needed on the way in or out of the network.

Same answer every time

The maths is exact, so the output can be predicted. In a test, 2.4 billion samples went through the board and back with zero differences.

Changes land cleanly

When you move a setting, the whole new set takes effect on one sample boundary. A frame is never half old and half new.

You can rewire it

The same board went from a small analog mixer to a 20-channel network and USB mixer by loading new designs, with no new hardware.

The honest trade-offs.

Designing a circuit is slower and harder than writing software, and every change needs a rebuild. Each sample has a fixed time budget (256 ticks). Conversation-heavy jobs like USB and network protocols are far easier on the computer half, which is why this board uses both.

7What is next: effects inserted into the chain

Planned, not built yet

EQ, dynamics and delay, added the way you add an insert to a channel strip.

The mixer is built as a chain of stages that all speak the same language. That lets a new stage be dropped in between two others. Three insert points are planned: on the inputs, on the buses and on the outputs.

  • No zipper noise. A smoother between a fader move and the maths will glide each change in over about a millisecond, so there are no clicks.
  • Sidechains. An effect such as a compressor will be able to listen to another channel or bus.
  • Room to grow. A rough estimate says 64 channels of 8-band EQ would take about 10 of the chip's 360 calculators.

8Glossary

FPGA
A chip made of building blocks you wire into any circuit you like. The design is a wiring diagram, not a program.
Clock tick
One beat of the chip's internal clock. There are 256 of them in each sample at 48 kHz.
Sample period
The time for one sample, 20.8 millionths of a second. All 20 channels are handled inside it.
Matrix / crosspoint
A grid that decides how much of each input goes to each output. Each cell is a crosspoint with its own gain.
Bus
A mix that several inputs are sent to, as on a console.
DSP slice
A tiny hardware calculator that multiplies two numbers and adds the result to a running total.
DMA
A hardware courier that moves data between memory and a device without the computer's help.
Buffer / queue
A short holding area that smooths out differences in timing between two parts.
AVB / gPTP
Network audio over Ethernet, and the shared clock that keeps every device on the network in step.
Bit-exact
Giving the identical result, to the last bit, every time, so that it can be checked against a reference.
Patch
The setting that picks which physical input or output each channel uses.

9A suggested talk flow

  1. What it is. The diagram in section 1. "A console's routing and faders on one chip."
  2. Two kinds of brain. The computer half and the FPGA half. Use the crackling-buffer example.
  3. One clock. The 256-tick sample bar. Word clock, then the network clock.
  4. Computer to FPGA. The buffer, courier and queue. Link it to their I/O buffer setting.
  5. Few workers, many jobs. The grid of 400 squares against 4 workers.
  6. Why an FPGA. Pick three tiles that suit the room, and show the honest trade-offs.
  7. What is next. Inserts, no zipper noise, sidechains. Say clearly that this part is planned.
